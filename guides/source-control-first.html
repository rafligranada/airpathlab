Foundations · GUIDE 02

Source control, ventilation and filtration: the right order

Three approaches solve different problems; most households need a mix, not a single gadget.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

First remove or reduce the source

If an activity creates an irritant, the most reliable step is often to prevent it from entering the air in the first place. Reduce unnecessary burning of candles or incense, follow product instructions for cleaning chemicals, and maintain appliances that generate fumes. An air cleaner may reduce particles already present, but cannot make a harmful source harmless.

FIELD NOTE 01The EPA places source control and ventilation before supplemental air cleaning for a reason: reducing what enters the room also reduces what every other strategy must handle.

Ventilate with appropriately clean outdoor air

Ventilation exchanges indoor and outdoor air. An exhausting range hood or bathroom fan can remove pollutants and moisture near their origin. Open windows can help when outdoor air is suitable and the home is safe to ventilate naturally. Mechanical systems may provide more predictable exchange than random openings.

FIELD NOTE 02Ventilation is context-dependent. During wildfire smoke or high outdoor pollution, freely opening windows may bring contaminants indoors; follow local public-health advisories.

Filter particles as a supplement

Portable air cleaners and compatible HVAC filters can remove particles passing through their filters. An air cleaner generally does not remove every gas or address poor ventilation on its own. Its rated particle CADR describes a particular test condition, typically at a higher fan speed than people may use at night.

FIELD NOTE 03A product labeled HEPA is not automatically sized for your space. Compare published CADR and actual room volume, then consider noise and run time.

Use the kitchen as a decision example

A frying pan creates cooking particles. The first question is whether the existing hood captures the plume and exhausts it appropriately. A nearby purifier may help remove residual particles, but it is not a substitute for capturing the source. Similarly, a musty basement needs moisture investigation, not only a larger filter.

FIELD NOTE 04Match each action to the mechanism. If the problem is an odor from a plumbing leak, a fan aimed at the carpet will not repair the water entry.

Avoid one-size-fits-all claims

There is no universal “best air purifier setting” or guaranteed ACH value that makes every room safe. The right action depends on the source, the building, the people and the outdoor conditions. Think in layers: limit the source, bring in cleaner air when appropriate, and add correctly sized filtration if helpful.

FIELD NOTE 05Document assumptions before spending money. A simple room map and one-week observation often show more than a product specification alone.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
