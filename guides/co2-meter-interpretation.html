Diagnosis · GUIDE 17

CO₂ readings: useful ventilation clues but not a full air-quality score

What to record and what not to infer from a consumer carbon dioxide monitor.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

Know what the instrument measures

A CO₂ meter may help indicate how occupied-space ventilation changes under similar conditions. It does not measure fine particles, many gases or biological hazards directly. Some inexpensive devices estimate CO₂ rather than measuring it; check the sensing method and specifications.

FIELD NOTE 01A single number cannot certify a room as safe or unsafe for all contaminants.

Use comparable conditions

Readings rise and fall with occupant count, activity, room volume, air exchange and sensor location. To compare two days, record number of people, window/door positions and HVAC mode. Avoid placing the sensor directly in an exhaled breath stream or immediately beside a window.

FIELD NOTE 02Record the time and whether the instrument was warmed up and calibrated according to instructions.

Do not treat a guideline as a diagnosis

There is no universally reliable “green CO₂ number” that proves good indoor air. The relevance depends on purpose and context. A purifier can reduce airborne particles without lowering CO₂. A good CO₂ reading does not rule out a local pollution source.

FIELD NOTE 03Avoid false reassurance from a screen displaying a reassuring color.

Make a sensible experiment

If the device is trustworthy, use it to compare how an existing ventilation mode influences a similarly occupied room over time. Note whether other conditions changed. Do not use data from a single inexpensive sensor to modify building systems or decide occupational code compliance.

FIELD NOTE 04A professional can measure actual airflows and evaluate ventilation design.

Preserve the main question

Start with “Why is this room uncomfortable?” rather than “How do I make the number smaller?” If cooking particles or a moisture leak are the problem, CO₂ is the wrong tool to identify the cause.

FIELD NOTE 05Use measurements to support, not replace, source investigation.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
