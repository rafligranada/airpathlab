Foundations · GUIDE 03

Trace the air path through a room without special instruments

Understand supply, return and exhaust points before changing furniture or adding fans.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

Identify the room’s pathways

Air usually enters and exits a space through more than one route: supply outlets, return grilles, door undercuts, leakage gaps and open windows. Identify which devices actively move air and which are only openings. A ceiling fan circulates room air but does not necessarily provide outdoor ventilation.

FIELD NOTE 01Mark supply and return locations on a diagram. The aim is to understand likely paths, not to claim you have measured actual airflow.

Look for blocked or short-circuited flow

A couch against a return grille or a closed door that disrupts a designed return path can affect comfort. Likewise, a portable cleaner tucked behind a curtain may be unable to draw or discharge air as intended. Avoid redirecting an HVAC supply into a return without contacting the occupied zone.

FIELD NOTE 02Never cover a vent to “force” airflow elsewhere without understanding system design: that can cause pressure and comfort issues.

Different rooms need different attention

The bedroom may need consistent overnight airflow and low noise. The kitchen benefits from capture at the cooking source. The bathroom needs moisture removal. A shared office may require balancing drafts against adequate fresh air. The most useful observation is where people are and what activities occur.

FIELD NOTE 03Use this as a checklist instead of ranking rooms by floor area alone.

Test one change at a time

If allowed by the equipment manual, reposition a free-standing cleaner with appropriate clearance and observe whether a visible plume or lingering particle source behaves differently. Avoid improvised smoke tests, open flames or chemical tracers indoors. A change in perceived draft is not a quantitative airflow reading.

FIELD NOTE 04Write down the original position before experimenting so you can revert if comfort gets worse.

Escalate equipment problems appropriately

Unusual equipment noise, unsafe electrical conditions, suspected combustion backdrafting or persistent condensation should prompt professional assessment. Airflow patterns depend on fan performance, pressure differences and duct layout that cannot be diagnosed reliably from an illustration.

FIELD NOTE 05A professional can measure pressure and flow. Your room sketch helps communicate what is happening without claiming to replace that measurement.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
