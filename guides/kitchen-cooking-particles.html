Rooms · GUIDE 09

Cooking particles: capture at the source before relying on a purifier

A decision guide for range hoods, open windows and lingering kitchen haze.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

Recognize the source

Searing, frying and some high-temperature cooking can generate particulate matter and odors. The amount depends on ingredients, temperature, ventilation and technique. Source reduction can include using appropriate heat, preventing food from burning and maintaining cooking equipment.

FIELD NOTE 01Never ignore smoke alarms or the smell of fuel gas; follow emergency guidance first.

Check what the range hood actually does

Some hoods exhaust to the outdoors; others recirculate air through a filter and return it to the room. These are not equivalent mechanisms. Follow the hood manual and keep any grease filters maintained. Use effective capture when cooking rather than only after particles spread.

FIELD NOTE 02A visually large hood does not guarantee good capture for every burner or pan. Installation and airflow require professional evaluation.

Choose a safe supplemental strategy

A correctly sized portable particle cleaner can assist with residual particles but should not be placed where grease, steam or heat will damage it. Do not position cords near hot cookware. Opening a window may help in some circumstances but can compete with hood airflow or admit polluted outdoor air.

FIELD NOTE 03A purifier with only a particle filter cannot guarantee removal of cooking gases.

Document a real cooking session

Record cooking method, duration, exhaust use, whether the hood vents outdoors, when haze appears and how long odors linger. Repeat with one variable changed, such as using an existing hood at a suitable setting from the start. This is not a laboratory air quality test, but it helps distinguish habit from equipment limitations.

FIELD NOTE 04If smoke routinely overwhelms your kitchen, consult a professional rather than assuming a higher-CADR device alone will solve capture issues.

Plan maintenance and upgrades carefully

Grease buildup and blocked vents can reduce performance and create a fire risk. Follow manufacturer maintenance instructions, and use qualified tradespeople for ducting or electrical changes. Choose upgrades based on your cooking routine and constraints of the building.

FIELD NOTE 05Keep the cooking source, exhaust path and filtration as distinct decisions instead of relying on one product label.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
