Start here · GUIDE 01

A 15-minute room air audit that separates symptoms from sources

Use a repeatable observation route before you buy a purifier or change a filter.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

Start with a room map

Draw a rough plan showing windows, doors, supply vents, exhaust grilles, fans and the main sources of particles or odors. Record the time of day and what happened just before the problem appeared. For example, a room that feels uncomfortable only after cooking needs a different investigation from one that stays stuffy overnight. The map turns a vague complaint into a location and time that can be compared later.

FIELD NOTE 01Do not infer air quality from smell alone: some pollutants have little odor, and a pleasant smell does not prove an environment is safe.

Separate three kinds of problems

Ask whether you are noticing a source, an airflow pathway or a filter issue. Smoke from a pan is a source. A range hood that does not exhaust outdoors may be a pathway or capture problem. A portable cleaner on its lowest speed is a filtration-capacity issue. These categories can overlap, but separating them prevents expensive, unrelated purchases.

FIELD NOTE 02A useful first action is often to change the activity that creates pollution, or to capture emissions where they start, before trying to clean the entire room.

Take a before-and-after observation

Choose one low-risk intervention: unobstruct a return grille, use an existing kitchen exhaust correctly, or move a portable air cleaner away from a curtain according to its manual. Keep other conditions as similar as possible. Record what changed, how long you waited and whether the improvement persisted. A single subjective observation is not laboratory proof; repeated observations reveal patterns.

FIELD NOTE 03If outdoor air is smoky or polluted, opening windows as a test can make things worse. Check outdoor conditions first.

Know when observations are not enough

Persistent moisture, visible mold growth, fuel-burning appliance concerns or unexplained symptoms require more than a casual room audit. Stop using equipment that seems unsafe and seek appropriate professional help. In particular, suspected carbon monoxide exposure is an emergency rather than a ventilation experiment.

FIELD NOTE 04Keep the written log even if you hire a professional: a timeline, room sketch and notes about when the issue appears are often more useful than a list of products you considered.

Turn the findings into a small plan

Name one likely source, one action you can take safely, one thing to measure and a date to check the result. For example: “The study seems dusty after vacuuming; try a well-maintained vacuum with suitable filtration and track visible settling for seven days.” If nothing changes, revisit the assumption rather than repeating the same fix.

FIELD NOTE 05Use the AirPath tools to estimate particle-cleaning capacity or record filter service dates, but remember that calculations cannot certify a room as healthy.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
