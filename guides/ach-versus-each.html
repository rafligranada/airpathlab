Filtration · GUIDE 05

ACH versus eACH: why “five air changes” needs context

Avoid mixing outdoor ventilation, particle cleaning and simplistic marketing claims.

AirPath Lab EditorialPublished and reviewed: 12 Nov 2026Reading time: 5–7 min
Safety boundary. This page explains observations and planning. It does not diagnose indoor pollution, replace building-code assessment or provide wiring, fuel-gas or mold-remediation instructions. Follow equipment manuals and seek qualified help for hazards.

Define a true outdoor-air change

Air changes per hour (ACH) commonly describes the flow of air through a volume relative to that volume. In a simplified ventilation calculation, airflow in m³/h divided by room volume in m³ gives ACH. Real buildings may have simultaneous supply, exhaust and leakage; measuring actual ventilation is more complex than reading a fan label.

FIELD NOTE 01Do not add supply and exhaust to double-count the same flow through a room. Professional guidance distinguishes different system arrangements.

Understand effective particle cleaning

An air cleaner does not create fresh outdoor air. Its particle CADR can be translated into an equivalent air-change number (eACH), which makes it easier to compare particle-cleaning rates. It does not imply the unit reduces carbon dioxide or all gases.

FIELD NOTE 02If a room is stuffy because of inadequate outdoor ventilation, adding a particle filter alone does not answer the original problem.

Why published targets vary

CDC has recommended aiming for five or more combined effective air changes in certain workplace respiratory-virus-risk contexts where feasible. That is not a universal residential code requirement or a promise that infection cannot occur. Occupancy, activity, airflow delivery and local regulations matter.

FIELD NOTE 03When a product claims “X air changes per hour,” check its assumed room size and whether the figure refers to recirculated, cleaned, or outdoor air.

Work a transparent example

Consider a 60 m³ room with a 240 m³/h portable cleaner. Simple particle eACH is 4. Suppose outdoor air were separately measured as 60 m³/h: simplified ventilation ACH would be 1. These two numbers serve different purposes; they should not be represented as 5 ACH of fresh air.

FIELD NOTE 04The estimates become less precise when spaces are poorly mixed or the cleaner runs below rated speed.

What to record in your plan

Write down source, units, room volume, device speed and whether a figure is measured or estimated. Use “estimated eACH from rated CADR” rather than “guaranteed five air changes.” This vocabulary helps prevent comparing marketing figures that have different meanings.

FIELD NOTE 05For compliance, healthcare or occupational design, seek a qualified specialist and use the applicable standard rather than a consumer calculator.

Practical next step

Choose one low-risk observation from this guide, write down when and where you tried it, and compare with a similar situation. If you cannot separate the cause from the result, pause before buying equipment. For a larger or persistent building problem, an in-person assessment may be the most useful next step.
